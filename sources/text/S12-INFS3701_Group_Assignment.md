INFS3701 Enterprise Architecture for Scalable Cloud Solutions Group Assignment: A Scalable, Highly Available Cloud Service Term 3, 2026 Weight 25 marks (25% of the course) Structure One member submits on behalf of the group. List every member and their contribution in your report. Scope Course content up to Week 9. The core spans networking (Week 7), databases (Week 8) and monitoring (Week 9). Going Further draws on anything else from Weeks 5-9. Due date Tuesday 17 November 2026 (Week 10), 4:00 pm, Sydney time Submission Moodle (all documents, codes and video)  
Assignment Description Building a scalable, highly available cloud service. Your group will design, build and deploy a cloud service that does not depend on any single server. Instead of one instance that fails under load, your solution must spread requests across multiple servers, keep working when a component is lost, and be built so that capacity can grow with demand. This is the capstone of the course. It draws on everything taught up to Week 9 (compute and load balancing, storage, networking, security, databases, and monitoring). The core requirements can all be met with material covered by Week 8; Week 9's monitoring and observability content is the natural way to go further. As always, the marks reward the reasoning behind your choices, not just a working result. Choosing Your Starting Point Your group may take either of two routes. Both are marked identically. Choose the one that suits your team. Option A: Scale up a member's individual service Pick one team member's individual assignment as your starting point and name it in your report. It already has compute, storage and an interface, so your effort goes into the architecture rather than into building an application. If that service is very simple, you may extend it over some aspects of the business problem, so there is something meaningful to scale. Option B: Start from a new idea Alternatively, choose a new problem and a new service that no member built for their individual assignment. This is a good option if your group wants a shared problem to work on rather than adopting one member's work. Keep the application deliberately small (you are assessed on the architecture, not on the size of the feature set) and make sure it still has real server-side work to do, persistent data and a usable interface. Whichever route you take, state it clearly in your design document. A front-end-only or static site does not meet the requirement under either option, and the worked examples used in class (the café, and the URL shortener from the tutorials) cannot be submitted as your own service. What Your Solution Must Do To meet the expected level for this course, your deployed solution must be genuinely scalable and highly available: • Distribute load: requests are spread across at least two compute instances behind a load balancer (or a serverless service that does this for you), so no single server is a bottleneck.

• Survive failure: the solution spans at least two Availability Zones and keeps working if one instance or one Availability Zone is lost. • Be built to scale out: the application tier is stateless, so capacity can be added simply by adding instances. Session and application state live in the data tier, not on the server. (Making the scaling happen automatically is Going Further; see below.) • Sound networking: a sensible VPC design with public and private subnets that exposes only what must be public. • A resilient managed database: your project must include one, either a relational database deployed across Availability Zones, or a managed NoSQL table. Object storage on its own does not satisfy this. (Week 8) • Monitoring you can show: A dashboard covering the health of your solution, and at least one alarm on a signal that matters, with evidence that it fired in a test. (Week 9) • Secured sensibly: least-privilege IAM roles, tightly scoped security groups, and encryption in transit and at rest. • Usable interface: the service remains reachable and usable through its interface. A single-instance design, a project with no managed database, or one with no monitoring does not meet the core requirement, however well the original service works. Going Further: Earning the Top Marks A correct, well-justified core earns a solid mark. To reach the Above Expectations band, go beyond it by completing either of the two options below, and show evidence that what you added works. • Automate the scaling. Add an Auto Scaling group with a scaling policy, so capacity follows demand without anyone intervening. This is Week 10 material, taught after the deadline: attempting it is genuinely going beyond and is rewarded as such. It is not required, and you are not penalised for leaving it out. Topics from Weeks 2 and 8 offer other ways to achieve automatic scaling. • Apply any three further topics from Weeks 5–9 that the core does not already require. Apply them to your solution and justify how each one covers at least one of the business needs. Topics you could draw on: • A proven failover test: Show that your design survives a failure. While your service is receiving steady traffic, deliberately stop one instance and record what happens: how long the load balancer took to stop sending traffic to it, and how many requests failed in that time. Include the evidence (a log of requests, screenshots or CloudWatch graphs) and explain the result, including why it isn't zero. Draws on Weeks 7, 8 and 9. • Encryption with AWS KMS, or credentials held in Secrets Manager (Week 5) • A refined identity design: The least-privilege policy you would attach, and why the lab's shared role is too broad (Week 5) • CloudTrail auditing, or AWS Config rules to catch configuration drift (Week 9) Whatever you choose, it must solve a problem or cover a business need that you can name. Three enhancements described and justified but not demonstrated will not reach the top band. How the Tutorials Build This You are not expected to do this in your own time alone. The assignment is released after the Week 5 lecture, and in that week's tutorial you form your group and choose your project. From Week 7 each tutorial teaches a technique on a worked example and then gives you a supervised session to apply it to your own project. Each of those three sessions delivers one part of the core: • Week 5. Form your group and choose your service and route (Option A or B). • Week 7. Design the VPC across two Availability Zones and sketch the first version of your architecture diagram.

Keeping Costs Under Control Your AWS Academy Learner Lab is a sandbox with a limited budget of credits, and a multi-instance, load-balanced stack consumes credits quickly. Protect your budget: • Build and test your solution, capture the evidence you need (screenshots and the working interface), then tear the stack down. Do not leave a multi-instance environment running. • Clicking “Reset” in the “Launch AWS Academy Learner Lab” menu deletes all of the configurations you have set up. • Choose small instance types; you are demonstrating an architecture, not running production traffic. • Track your remaining credits. Using AI to Build Your Solution As in the individual assignment, you are expected to use AI assistants to help with configuration and code, and you do not need a paid subscription. The tutorials cover which free tools to use and how to prompt in practice. Keep screenshots with the evidence of your progress. What matters is that your group understands and can explain the architecture; the design document and video assess your reasoning. Deliverables Five graded deliverables, plus a contribution statement and a group cover sheet: 1. Design document (PDF) A design document, under 4,000 words AND under 8 pages (references and appendices excluded), submitted as a PDF. It must address each of the following: • Solution objective: the route you chose (a member's individual service, or a new idea), the problem it solves, and what you set out to achieve. • Architecture & services used: every AWS service in your solution, and how the pieces fit together (refer to your diagram). • Scalability: how your design distributes load and how capacity can grow, and why. • High availability: how your design survives the loss of an instance or an Availability Zone, and why. • Networking, security & data: your VPC, security and data-tier decisions, with reasoning. • Cost: how you kept the solution within your Learner Lab credits, and the basis of your 12-month estimate. • Going further (if attempted): the monitoring and any other enhancements you added, why, and the evidence they work.
